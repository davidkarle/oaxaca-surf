Log a session: this is what makes it beat Surfline

After you surf (or watch), enter the face height of the biggest sets: it's compared with the top of the app's range. Add the top of Surfline's range for that hour too. Every report corrects the whole coast's size level and this spot's own factor (plus a period term, since long-period swell is where generic models miss most here), and the app tracks its error against Surfline's.

How the prediction works

1. Deep-water swell, four models. For each spot the app pulls the swell partitions (primary, secondary, tertiary swell plus wind sea) from ECMWF WAM, NOAA GFS-Wave, Météo-France MFWAM and DWD GWAM through Open-Meteo, at a grid point well offshore. Single-model apps inherit that model's bias; here each model is run through the same transform and you see the spread.

2. Refraction over real bathymetry. For every spot and wave period, about 1,400 wave rays are traced backward over the GEBCO 2026 depth grid (15 arc-second, ~460 m), from ~10 m depth off the break out to deep water. Rays that hit land are blocked. Wave action is conserved along each ray, so the table gives the height factor a deep-water swell from each direction delivers to the spot. That is how the Puerto Escondido canyon's focusing on long-period SSW swell at Zicatela, the shelter at La Punta and Barra's preference for S–SE all come out of the seafloor rather than guesses. Deep-water directional spread is assumed (narrow for long-period groundswell, broad for wind sea).

2b. Full spectra (GFS-Wave spectral). When the app runs from tools/serve.py, it also uses NOAA's full GFS-Wave 2D spectra (50 frequencies × 36 directions, hourly) from the two output stations off Oaxaca, blended by distance. Every frequency/direction bin is multiplied by its own ray-traced refraction factor, so no swell is summarised into one period and the long-period forerunners that the Puerto canyon amplifies are counted properly. The bins' real directional spread replaces the assumed one. It appears as its own model, "GFS-Wave spectral".

2c. Hurricane swell. Global wave models are too coarse to resolve a hurricane's small, intense core, so swell from storms near Mexico often comes in bigger than forecast. For each active eastern Pacific storm the app reads NHC's forecast advisory (track, intensity, 4 m seas radii) and runs Young's (1988) parametric hurricane wave model hour by hour along the track: radius of max wind from Willoughby & Rahn (2004), an equivalent fetch that includes the "trapped fetch" boost when the storm moves at about the waves' group speed, then JONSWAP growth. Swell decays with distance as √(r₀/r), with r₀ fitted so the height matches NHC's 4 m-seas radius toward the spot, and arrives after distance ÷ group speed. It then goes through the same refraction and breaking steps. It's shown as its own dashed line, not mixed into the ensemble.

3. Breaking height. Komar & Gaughan (1972): Hb = 0.39·g0.2·(T·H0²)0.4. This is the piece generic forecasts underweight: a 1 m, 18 s swell breaks bigger than a 1 m, 10 s swell. Partitions combine as energy (root-sum-square).

4. Your calibration. A report is the face of the biggest sets, so it's compared with the top of the app's range (1.25× its central value, about the ratio of the biggest tenth of waves to the biggest third). All reports are fitted together as ln(observed / predicted sets) = coast-wide level + spot factor + period term. The coast-wide level is shared by every spot, so a report at one spot also corrects the others when the whole forecast runs high or low. It starts at zero and counts like half a report, so it follows your reports quickly. Each spot's factor starts at zero, except at Zicatela: the Puerto Escondido canyon that makes it the biggest spot on the coast isn't in any public depth data (GEBCO shows a smooth shelf there), so Zicatela starts at ×1.42 from local knowledge (about 1.5× Plataforma on typical swell, more on long-period). The prior counts like about two reports, so your own sessions take over quickly. The same reports also score each wave model: every report keeps each model's own forecast for that hour, and models whose forecasts land closer to what was observed get more weight in the ensemble (a weighted median), all spots pooled since a model's offshore bias is shared along the coast. Scores start neutral and move gradually (a starting value worth three reports), so it takes several sessions before one model dominates. The leave-one-out error is shown next to Surfline's so you can see which is closer on your own data.

Wind is the mean of ECMWF IFS, GFS and ICON at the beach, classified against each spot's offshore direction. Tide is Open-Meteo's sea level (includes tide). All spot parameters, including coordinates, are editable in the spot panel.

Limits, stated plainly: I couldn't find a public real-time wave buoy off Oaxaca, so there's no buoy correction; your logged reports play that role. Coordinates for Puertecito and Plataforma are estimates from listings and travel times. GEBCO is coarse near shore (the 10 m start points land 1–3 km off the beach), so it captures the canyon and shelf but not sandbars, reef or the last few hundred metres of a point; the per-spot calibration from your reports covers that. The app's accuracy over Surfline only becomes real after you log a few sessions per spot.
